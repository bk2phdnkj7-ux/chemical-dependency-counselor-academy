Models of Addiction Overlook

One person. One story. Four very different ways of explaining addiction. At this overlook, your job is not to choose a “winning” model. Your job is to notice what each lens helps us see—and what it may leave outside the frame.

Trail Briefing

The assigned Open Oregon reading presents four models that are influential in U.S. society: the moral view, disease model, public health perspective, and sociological model. Each model changes the questions we ask, where we locate the problem, and what kinds of responses may seem appropriate.

Meet Alex: Your Case at the Overlook

Alex is 34 and has been using opioids regularly. The pattern intensified after a workplace injury led to chronic pain and time away from work. Alex has recently lost stable employment, feels increasingly isolated, and has returned to opioid use after periods of stopping. Alex says, “People keep telling me I should just get it together, but they don’t understand what this feels like.”

Trail question: What changes when we look at the same story through four different lenses?

Four Viewpoints at the Overlook

1. Moral View

This view frames illicit drug use and addiction in terms of right and wrong, sin, or personal failing. The reading notes that versions of this view can appear in some faith-based programs and in criminalization and punishment-oriented responses to drug use.

What might this lens notice?

It may emphasize individual conduct, personal responsibility, adherence to rules or values, and behavioral change.

What should a helping professional watch for?

When moral judgment is applied to a client, it can contribute to shame or stigma. A counselor should distinguish a client's own values and sources of meaning from imposing judgment on the client.

2. Disease Model

This model understands addiction as a disease. The reading traces its history and describes the later framing of addiction as a chronic, relapsing brain disease or disorder. It also discusses medication such as methadone as part of treatment for opioid dependence.

What might this lens notice?

It directs attention toward biological and neurological processes, dependence, changes associated with long-term use, and medical treatment.

What can this lens miss if used alone?

The reading notes critiques that a disease-only explanation may underemphasize the social context in which addiction develops, is identified, and is experienced.

3. Public Health Perspective

This perspective focuses on protecting health and reducing drug-related harm. The reading connects it with harm reduction, including syringe exchange and naloxone distribution, rather than making abstinence a prerequisite for every health intervention.

What might this lens ask first?

What can reduce immediate risk, prevent injury or death, and help this person stay as healthy and safe as possible?

What misconception does the reading address?

The chapter discusses the claim that harm reduction enables drug use and contrasts it with evidence that interventions such as syringe exchange and naloxone distribution reduce serious harms.

4. Sociological Model

This model examines how social structures, institutions, inequality, policy, and environments shape drug use and drug-related harm. The chapter discusses risk environments, economic distress, working conditions, social capital, and other structural factors.

What might this lens notice in Alex's story?

The workplace injury, employment loss, chronic pain, isolation, economic pressure, access to services, and the larger environment surrounding Alex's substance use.

Why does context matter?

A sociological analysis asks what becomes invisible when we look only at the individual or the drug and ignore the social environment around them.

The Overlook: Put the Lenses Together

The chapter presents four distinct social models, but professional practice benefits from noticing that a person's substance use can have biological, behavioral, relational, cultural, environmental, and structural dimensions at the same time. No single label tells us everything about Alex.

Field note: Models are maps. A map can help you notice important terrain, but it is not the person standing in front of you.

Final Checkpoint: Pack Your Binoculars

Why can the model we use affect treatment and policy?

Models influence what we define as the cause of a problem, who or what we believe should respond, and whether the response emphasizes punishment, medical care, harm reduction, or changes to social conditions.

Which model explicitly emphasizes harm reduction?

The public health perspective.

Which model emphasizes social structures and risk environments?

The sociological model.

What is the key professional takeaway?

Stay curious about the lens being used. Avoid reducing a person to a moral judgment, diagnosis, behavior, or social condition. Understanding multiple dimensions can support more thoughtful, person-centered responses.
